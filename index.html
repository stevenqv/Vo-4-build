Fundamentals of Drawing

Every artist need to start somewhere, so the first thing you need to do is to understand the key principles of what makes a drawing excellent. Here are some you need to know:

Lines

Lines are the backbone of every drawings ever, even lineless ones. Some of the most recognizable lines are:

  • Straight Lines
  • Wavy Lines
  • Curved Lines
  • Dotted/Dashed Lines
  • Zigzagged Lines

Shapes

After the lines are intersected, shapes are created. They are essential for building objects and characters in figure sketches as well as backgrounds. They comes in two types:

  • 2D Shapes: Flat forms that does not have any depths shown (circles, squares, and triangles).
  • 3D Shapes: Forms with depth that are essential to constructing complex subjects like items and characters (spheres, cubes, and cylinders).

Values

Values not only define the light and dark areas of a drawing, but also what makes it three-dimensional in the first place.

  • Tint (lighter hue)
  • Tones (grey variant of hue)
  • Shade (darker hue)

Textures and Shading Techniques

Textures define how the drawing should feel. One way to express this was through shading techniques, particularly when using only one color. Here are some common techniques to consider:

  • Hatching: Single directional lines
  • Cross-hatching: Multiple and intersecting directional lines for rough effects
  • Stippling: Little dots on varying spaces
  • Blending: Smooth shading through smudging lines to emulate a smoother feel
  • Scribbling: Random yet mostly continuous line to portray an energetic feel

Color Theory

Before you start coloring, please understand what a color theory is, including color harmonies, to make the drawing look more appealing to the viewers.

  • Primary Colors: Red, Yellow, and Blue
  • Secondary Colors: Green, Orange, and Purple

Notable Color Harmonies

  • Complementary Colors: Opposite hues on the color wheels
  • Analogues Colors: Neighboring colors on the color wheel
  • Monochromatic Color: Single color with varying values

7 Principles of Drawing

Finally, with the essential knowledge of what makes a drawing, now you need to understand how the drawing looks good according to its principles:

  • Proportions
  • Balance
  • Contrast
  • Emphasis
  • Unity
  • Rhythm
  • Movement

Subject and Background Drawings

Now that you recognize the key features of what makes a drawing look good, you'll apply them to construct any subjects cleanly from objects to characters to even backgrounds!

Subject Drawings

Here are some key features and tips on how to draw subjects in general.

  • Observation: The most important step on drawing proportion-accurate subjects is to study them carefully, mapping out not only their structures, but also important landmarks you might start with.
  • Simplification: On the next step, break down the subject into simple shapes devoid of details so you have an easier time to draw. Methods can include rough sketch and blocking.
  • Repetition: Try drawing the same subject multiple times so you can remember not only its form, but also how it is visually built from, such knowing what 3D shapes to apply on.

Figure Drawings

In a nutshell, figure drawings are sketches of characters, but it can also extends to living organisms like animals. Here are some key features and tips specifically on them (they can also apply to animals as well):

  • Anatomy: Before you get started, learn how the organism's body work, marking landmarks that will immensely help you with drawing accurately.
  • Simple Forms: Considering that humans are abstractly made up of cylinders and spheres, focus on those shapes instead (unless you prefer boxes for torso and crotch for perspective purpose)
  • Gestures: Only focus more on drawing the organism's movements than the organisms themselves.

Backgrounds

After you are done mastering drawing focal subjects from objects to characters, now it's time to focus on the background.

  • Composition: Setting up the scene is the first step to making a background so the overall drawing, especially if the drawing's landscape-focused, feels more appealing to look at on a visual aspect.
  • Perspective: This is very essential because it maps out a guideline for the background to add simple shapes before preparing for complex ones. There are multiple types of perspectives you can experiment with before finalizing your decision.

Animations

After you are done with both sections above, now you are ready to go beyond drawing and start animating scenes through drawing it multiple times with alterations to make it come alive!

The 12 Principles of Animations

According to Ollie Johnson and Frank Thomas in their book called "The Illusion of Life" on 1981, there are 12 principles that makes up a great animations.

  1. Squash and Stretch
  2. Anticipation
  3. Staging
  4. Straight-Ahead Action and Pose-to-Pose
  5. Follow Through and Overlapping Actions
  6. Ease In, Ease Out
  7. Arcs
  8. Secondary Actions
  9. Timing
  10. Exaggeration
  11. Solid Drawing
  12. Appeal

For more information on these principles, visit an Adobe's article on the 12 Principles of Animation.

Animation Features and Techniques

Now that you understand the 12 principles of animation, now you need to know how animation actually works in the workspace. There's also tips alongside for help when animating subjects.

  • Onion-skinning: An important animation technique that allows you to predict which frame to draw next by viewing frames before and after the selected frame.
  • Key Frames: A feature in any animations that marks key poses for the subject. The frames between key ones are called in-betweens.
  • Frame Rate: How fast and therefore smooth the frames goes when the animation plays.
  • Rotoscoping (2D) and Motion Capture (3D): A useful animation technique that can help map out the movements for subjects when animating, especially when people and animals are involved.
